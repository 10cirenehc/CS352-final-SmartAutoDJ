When a DJ transitions from one song to the next, the goal is for the audience to barely notice the change. The energy should keep moving, the beat should stay steady, and the two songs should feel like one continuous piece of music. Most automatic DJ systems that are popular right now like Spotify DJ, Apple Music AutoMix, or rekordbox, are mostly just crossfading. While that method is incredibly reliable, the problem with it is that basic crossfade does not really understand the music. It is not aware where the beats are, so the two songs can drift out of sync. It also does not know the structure of the songs, so it might bring in song B during a quiet intro while song A is still at a high-energy chorus, which drops the overall energy in a playlist. Since both songs are also playing loudly in the middle of the fade, the bass can stack up and make the transition sound muddy. Our goal was to build a transition system that analyzes both songs first, including tempo, downbeats, loudness, structure, and musical key, then uses that information to create a smoother handoff.

SmartAutoDJ takes two songs, A and B, and generates a short transition between them. We built the system in three tiers, where each tier adds on top of the previous one so we can compare what each feature improves. Tier 1 is the simple crossfade, which acts as our baseline comparison. Tier 2 is beat-aligned. It chooses a more musical exit point in song A and entry point in song B, time-stretches song B to match song A's tempo, aligns their downbeats to the same grid, and reshapes the overlap using equal-power fades. It also includes a bass-frequency swap so the low end does not double up. More technical details are included in the How it works section. Tier 3 adds an optional AI-generated bridge, such as a riser, drum fill, or sweep, using Meta's MusicGen-style model. While this model is not the best performing model as it is from 2023, we chose it for its open source nature (making it cost efficient) and the option to give it a reference audio. This bridge is mixed on top of the Tier 2 transition to add more creativity and energy to the transition. Importantly, the bridge is only additive. It improves the transition but does not replace the main algorithmic transition. Each part of the system also connects to course concepts, including loudness and amplitude, Fourier analysis, convolution and filtering, self-similarity and structure, and chroma/key estimation.

Beat and downbeat tracking uses beat_this, a neural (transformer) tracker, by default; the rest of the analysis including tempo, structure/novelty, key and all the fade/EQ/stretch DSP are built on librosa and scipy. The generative model runs separately on a cloud GPU through Modal because its dependencies conflict with the analysis environment. Our test set is a small collection of steady-tempo pop, EDM, and hip-hop clips with clear beats that we picked. For every rendered transition, the system also saves a JSON file with the detected tempos, beat and downbeat times, selected transition region, and evaluation metrics. This makes the results easier to inspect and reproduce. We evaluate each tier using three objective metrics measured only over the transition region. The first is beat-alignment error, which measures the average timing gap between matched beats from songs A and B. The second is tempo-match quality, which measures how much the BPM difference shrinks after stretching. The third is loudness continuity, which measures the largest sudden dB jump across the transition. Finally, we thoroughly evaluated the generated audio subjectively through our own judgement.

The clearest objective improvement is in loudness continuity. For example, on the Skrillex to Core transition, the naive crossfade creates a 12.6 dB jump at the transition. The beat-aligned Tier 3 transition reduces this to about 1.0 dB, so the volume moves much more smoothly instead of suddenly lurching. Tempo matching also works well when the songs are close enough in BPM. We cap stretching at about a 10% tempo difference so the audio does not sound unnaturally warped. Beat-alignment error is harder to report cleanly. It performs well for aligned transitions, but it is sensitive to cue placement on full tracks and is not very meaningful for short quick-cut demos where the songs barely overlap. Thus, we treat loudness continuity as our main result and interpret the other metrics together with the plots. We found that it was quite difficult to quantify how good a transition sounds, especially with the AI generated clip, which may be mixed in, perfectly aligned, but still sound weird. We also found that the MusicGen model has poor prompt adherence and large variance between runs, which is documented in their reporting as well. Thus, sometimes we had to judge multiple generations and pick better ones.

Under the hood

How it works, in detail.

The four mechanisms that make a SmartAutoDJ transition more than a crossfade. Expand any card for the signal-processing specifics.

Cue points How we pick where to transition

We do not simply overlap the end of song A with the start of song B. Instead, both transition points have to land on downbeats, and more specifically on a phrase grid, meaning every fourth downbeat. This is because real musical sections usually begin on phrase boundaries, not randomly in the middle of a bar.

For choosing the entry point in song B, our default energy-based method walks through this phrase grid and finds the first downbeat where the next two bars have energy above about 60% of B's peak phrase energy. In other words, it looks for the first point where song B has actually "kicked in." This helps avoid transitions that drop into a quiet or empty intro.

We also built a second, more experimental method that scores each candidate downbeat using a weighted combination:

0.5 × energy + 0.3 × novelty + 0.2 × harmonic compatibility

The novelty term tries to detect structural changes in the music. To do this, we compute frame-to-frame changes, or flux, across four feature streams: MFCCs for timbre, chroma for harmony, onset strength for rhythm, and RMS for loudness. We standardize these streams, average them together, and treat large spikes as possible section boundaries.

The harmonic compatibility term estimates the key of each song using a Krumhansl-Schmuckler chroma correlation. Then it scores how well song A and song B fit together using the Camelot wheel. Same key gets the highest score, relative major or minor gets a slightly lower score, and the score decreases as the keys move farther apart on the circle of fifths.

For choosing the exit point in song A, we take the latest downbeat that still leaves enough room for the full overlap. If there is a section boundary nearby, we nudge the exit point toward it. We also prefer bars that are still energetic, so song A exits at a strong musical moment instead of fading out weakly.

EQ fade Equal-power fade and bass swap

The overlap is not just a simple linear fade. We split each song into a low band and a high band using a fourth-order Butterworth filter at 200 Hz. We use zero-phase filtering with sosfiltfilt, and we compute the high band as the original signal minus the low band, so the two bands add back to the original signal exactly.

The high bands are mixed using an equal-power crossfade. Song A is multiplied by cos(t · π/2) and song B is multiplied by sin(t · π/2), where t goes from 0 to 1 across the transition. Since cos² + sin² = 1, the total power stays much more constant. This helps avoid the dip in the middle that often happens with a basic linear fade.

The low bands use a separate bass swap. Instead of letting both basslines play through the whole overlap, song A's bass holds for the first part, then hands off to song B's bass over a short window in the middle. This uses the same equal-power idea. The goal is that there is basically only one bassline dominating at any moment, so the kick drums do not stack on top of each other and make the mix muddy.

An earlier version faded both basslines down near the midpoint, but that created an obvious bass dropout. The equal-power bass handoff fixes this by preserving low-frequency energy while still avoiding bass buildup.

Swoosh The sweeping swoosh effect

For quick-cut transitions, we add a "swoosh" effect that builds tension right before the drop. The effect starts with white noise, then passes it through a swept resonant band-pass filter. The center frequency rises exponentially from about 200 Hz to about 12 kHz over the length of the effect.

The filter is a TPT, or topology-preserving transform, state-variable filter based on the Zavalishin design. In this filter, the cutoff is prewarped using g = tan(π · fc / sr) and the two integrators are updated using a trapezoidal method.

This matters because the ordinary Chamberlin state-variable filter can become unstable above about one-sixth of the sample rate, which is around 7 kHz at 44.1 kHz. That is a problem because our swoosh needs to sweep into the high-frequency range. The TPT version stays stable much closer to Nyquist, so the resonant peak can safely rise up to 12 kHz.

We set the resonance using k = 1/Q with Q = 8, which creates a narrow, vocal-like peak that is easy to hear as it rises. We also apply a rising amplitude envelope, t^1.5, so the swoosh grows louder as it climbs. After that, we peak-normalize the effect and loudness-match it to about 3 dB below the outgoing track's tail.

Finally, the first bar of song B crossfades in over the tail of the swoosh using another equal-power crossfade. This lets song B arrive on its downbeat without creating a hard click.

AI bridge How the AI bridge is mixed in

For Tier 3, we first create a boundary reference, which is a short equal-power crossfade from the tail of song A into the head of song B. We send this reference, along with an auto-generated text prompt containing the mix BPM, key, and duration, to a MusicGen-style model running on a cloud GPU through Modal. The model then returns a generated clip that is conditioned on the actual sound of the two songs.

After the clip comes back, we process it locally. First, we beat-align it to the mix tempo, with octave-folding guards so that a wrong tempo estimate does not stretch it in an extreme way. Second, if the clip is supposed to be a riser or sweep, we "riserize" it in post-processing. The model does not always create a clean rising sound by itself, so we force that effect by crossfading from a dull low-passed version of the clip into the brighter original version, low · (1 − t) + clip · t. This makes the bridge open from muffled to bright, so it peaks at the very end and lands right on the drop. Third, we trim or pad the clip to exactly match the seam length and peak-normalize it to about −9 dBFS.

The AI bridge is always mixed as an extra layer. It never replaces the main transition. We loudness-match it to about 3 dB below the main tracks, soft-limit its peaks using a smooth tanh curve, and duck the songs by about 6 dB underneath it where the bridge is loudest. We also carve out some space in the songs in the same frequency bands where the bridge is active, so the mix does not become muddy.

At the end, a final headroom check scales the whole output down if it would ever exceed 0.99 full scale. This keeps the transition from clipping.

Genre How genre is detected and used

SmartAutoDJ also detects the genre of each song and uses it to guide the transition. We use distilHuBERT, a smaller and faster version of the HuBERT audio transformer, with a checkpoint already fine-tuned on GTZAN for music genre classification. To keep it efficient, we take about 30 seconds from the middle of each song, convert it to 16 kHz mono, and run the model on the CPU. The model gives a probability for each genre, and we save both the top genre label and the full distribution.

After detecting the genres, we map them into broader style families like dance, urban, dub, band, smooth, or ambient. These style families control settings such as overlap length, allowed tempo-stretch range, whether to use bass swap, and which cue-point method to use. Genre also helps choose the AI bridge style: dance maps to a riser, hip-hop to a drum-and-stab fill, reggae to a dub siren, and ambient to a pad wash. The MusicGen prompt is filled with the detected BPM, key, and transition length, usually asking for an isolated effect instead of a full melody.
